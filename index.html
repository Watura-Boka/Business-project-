01 / THE FOUNDATIONS

Needs vs. wants

The distinction is simple, but it shapes every market.

NEEDS

Essential for living: water, food, shelter and clothing.

WANTS

Not essential to survive: mobile phones, cars, holidays and gaming consoles.

People’s wants can be unlimited: get one thing and they may want something else.

02 / SCARCITY & CHOICE

Scarcity: the problem behind every choice

Resources are limited. People’s wants are unlimited.

LIMITED RESOURCES

Time, money, workers, materials and machinery are not unlimited.

UNLIMITED WANTS

Get one thing and people may want something else.

THE ECONOMIC PROBLEM

People, businesses and governments must decide how to use limited resources.

There are not enough resources to produce everything everyone wants.

02 / SCARCITY & CHOICE

Opportunity cost

The next best alternative you give up when you make a choice.

FATIMA

Uses her $5,000 to start a cake business. She gives up saving it or buying something else.

A BUSINESS

Buys new machinery instead of training workers.

A GOVERNMENT

Builds a school instead of a hospital.

Opportunity cost = the next best alternative given up.

04 / EFFICIENCY

Specialisation vs. division of labour

Related ideas, but not exactly the same.

SPECIALISATION

Focusing on one particular task, skill, product or type of work. Example: a business makes only footballs.

DIVISION OF LABOUR

Production is split into separate tasks, with different workers doing each task. Example: different workers cut, stamp and stitch football panels.

IGCSE BUSINESS STUDIES • CHAPTER 1

04 / EFFICIENCY

A football production line

Each worker handles a different stage: cut panels, stamp the design, stitch, insert the bladder, shape and inflate.

BENEFITS

Faster, more skilled workers. More output in less time. Higher productivity and efficiency. Lower cost per unit, so lower prices are possible.

LIMITATIONS

Repetitive, boring work. One stalled stage slows everyone. Workers need training for their task.

05 / PRODUCTS & SERVICES

What does a business provide?

Goods are physical products. Services are activities you cannot physically touch.

DURABLE GOODS

Used many times: computers, TVs, cars, tables.

NON-DURABLE GOODS

Used up quickly or once: food and drinks.

CONSUMER SERVICES

Provided to the public: banking, insurance, bus journeys.

IGCSE BUSINESS STUDIES • CHAPTER 1

05 / PRODUCTS & SERVICES

Consumer good or capital good?

The same product can belong to different categories depending on its use.

A COMPUTER AT HOME

Bought by a family for personal use. Consumer good.

A COMPUTER AT WORK

Bought by a company to help run the business. Capital good.

Capital goods: machinery, computers and delivery vehicles sold to businesses to produce other goods and services.

IGCSE BUSINESS STUDIES • CHAPTER 1

06 / CREATING VALUE

Added value: make the product worth more

Added value = selling price − cost of bought-in materials/components.

INGREDIENTS

Flour, eggs, sugar and butter

→

BAKERY

Turns them into a cake

→

SELLING PRICE

More than the ingredients cost

Added value is not profit: wages, rent and electricity still have to be paid.

IGCSE BUSINESS STUDIES • CHAPTER 1

06 / CREATING VALUE

How can a business increase added value?

Give customers a reason to value the product more.

BRANDING

Build a recognised name or image.

EXCELLENT SERVICE

Offer personal, helpful, high-quality service.

PRODUCT FEATURES

Add useful functions or features.

CONVENIENCE

Save time or effort, such as ready-made meals.

Profit is what remains after all costs, including wages, rent and electricity, are deducted.
